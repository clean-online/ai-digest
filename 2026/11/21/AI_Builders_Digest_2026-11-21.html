Superintelligence Needs the Physical World with CEO and Co-Founder of Arda Bob McGrew

The Takeaway: superintelligence will not arrive from automating researchers alone, because the bottleneck is physical, not digital. Bob McGrew, CEO and co-founder of Arda, previously an early executive at Palantir and chief research officer at OpenAI, argues that a decade of ChatGPT and agents has changed our digital lives while leaving 'the atoms of our lives' almost untouched. 'I don't think you are actually gonna be able to get to superintelligence without changes in the physical world.' His example is the Waymo: amazing the first time, and by the third time you are complaining about traffic, because the car itself and the physical barriers around it never changed.

He takes aim at the hard-takeoff argument he says he has heard since 2018: automate 10%, 50%, 90%, 99% of a researcher's time and you go 2x, 10x, 100x faster into a runaway self-improvement loop. 'Researchers are not the bottleneck.' Even when he was chief research officer at OpenAI in 2024, the limit was not how many researchers could be hired but 'the amount of GPUs that we had on hand', whether compute was in the right places and connected by the right networks. Automating researchers is a big one-time gain, comparable to the jump to reasoning models, but it is another S-curve that moves GPUs from roughly 35% toward 90% of optimal; 90% to 99% is not a big difference and does not start a self-improvement cycle. He recalls Ilya listening patiently to a cool new method and asking 'so what are the results?', because deep learning is an experimental science and you have to run the experiments.

The economic point is that intelligence and the physical layer are complements: labs produce cheap intelligence, so when hypotheses become cheap the scarce part becomes the wet labs and the GPUs, and 'you're just gonna bang harder and harder against the limited number of wet labs that you have'. Better protein simulations cut the number of experiments tenfold, but the digital part is so fast that the gain is eaten up and you are blocked on the physical world again. An O-ring production function means you have to do all the steps: speeding up the digital part of building housing does little when the hard part is permits that 'no amount of agents are gonna help you do'. Want more data centers? You need bulldozers; to build a bulldozer factory you must clear the ground; to clear the ground you need more bulldozers.

Arda targets American manufacturing. The iPhone is designed in California and made in China, and he says each year about 75% of its parts change while only about 25% stay the same, so the stable quarter runs on a highly automated line and the rest is assembled by hand. A car plant can spend a couple of years and $200,000,000 on automation and then build roughly the same car for seven years; an iPhone would need to go from about 25% to about 90% automated to pencil out, and the bespoke machines cannot generalize even to the next generation. Around a million people assemble iPhones or components in Asia alongside about 10,000 manufacturing engineers. Arda's goal is to let you upload a product design to its cloud and design or reconfigure an automated line in seven days, as easily as retraining a human worker in a day; manufacturers consider seven days to bring a design change into production extremely agile.

He does not think the answer is a humanoid: 'two arms and a camera is enough', sometimes one arm and a camera, because assembly lines are a closed world with no novelty, unlike cleaning an unfamiliar bedroom. Existing arms can already build about 90% of these products; what blocks them is economics, since the capital equipment has to be redesigned and rebuilt every year. Half the work in a traditional automation cell is calibration, which perception and RL can relax, but a closed world still needs the right device 99 or 99.9% of the time. Arda spent its first year with defense manufacturer Androl building a software and AI platform: access to their systems in January, a first user in April who cut an actuator's virtual build from a few days to ninety minutes, then deployment across all product divisions from drones to missiles and work on the Fury collaborative combat airplane. Arda does not train its own models; frontier models act as an orchestrator over motion planning and CAD-inspection tools, and the team first had to build the equivalent of an IDE and Git for manufacturing engineers before an agent could add value.

His route there ran through a Stanford AI lab PhD he left in 2005 believing neural networks would not work, ten years at Palantir, a year and a half trying and failing to start a robotics company, and OpenAI's robotics team, where a shadow hand spent two years learning to solve a Rubik's cube, after which he says he is 'permanently not a believer in robotic hands'. He credits Palantir's forward deployed engineering model, noting he would not build a feature without a customer ready to use it, that a single power user such as Sarah Adams carried the product for about a year, and that the approach only has leverage when what the FTEs see is productized rather than left as consulting. On robotics he suspects models like Astra or Fable work because they learn good representations by 'predicting the last few significant bits' of real-world data, not because of a flood of robot data, and their slow speed and lack of fine-tuning leave room for specialists. He closes on Dario's 'a country of geniuses in a data center', arguing that perception and action are what let intelligence leave the data center, that the next ten years bring a profusion of robot forms rather than only humanoids, and that a civilization spreading at light speed explains the Fermi Paradox: 'We're only gonna detect them a little bit before they arrive. Or perhaps we will be the wave.' Figures and spellings here are as the conversation gives them; the feed does not independently verify them.

核心结论：只靠自动化研究员到不了超级智能，因为瓶颈在物理世界，不在数字世界。Arda 联合创始人兼 CEO Bob McGrew，此前是 Palantir 早期高管、OpenAI 首席研究官。他的判断是，ChatGPT 和 agent 这十年改变了我们的数字生活，却几乎没动到「我们生活里的原子」：'I don't think you are actually gonna be able to get to superintelligence without changes in the physical world.' 他举的例子是 Waymo：第一次坐觉得神奇，第三次就开始抱怨堵车，因为车本身和周围的物理限制一点没变。

他反驳的是自己说从 2018 年就听到的硬起飞论证：把研究员 10%、50%、90%、99% 的时间自动化，就能 2 倍、10 倍、100 倍地加速，进入失控的自我改进循环。「研究员不是瓶颈。」即便 2024 年他在 OpenAI 做首席研究官时，限制也不是能招多少研究员，而是「手上有多少 GPU」、算力有没有放在对的位置、有没有用对的网络连起来。自动化研究员是一次性大收益，堪比当年跃迁到推理模型，但它只是一条把 GPU 从大约 35% 推到 90% 最优的 S 曲线；90% 到 99% 差别不大，也不会启动自我改进循环。他记得 Ilya 耐心听完一个很妙的新方法，只问一句 'so what are the results?'，因为深度学习是实验科学，实验必须真跑。

经济上的道理是：智能和物理层是互补品。实验室生产的是便宜的智能，所以当假设变便宜，稀缺的就变成湿实验室和 GPU，「你会越来越狠地撞在湿实验室数量有限这堵墙上」。蛋白质模拟做得更好，实验次数能降到十分之一，但数字那一侧太快，这点加速很快被吃掉，又被物理世界卡住。O-ring 生产函数意思是每一步都得做全：把建房的数字部分提速没什么用，难的是拿许可，而「再多的 agent 也帮不了你拿许可」。想多建数据中心？需要推土机；要建推土机工厂，得先清地；要清地，又需要更多推土机。

Arda 做的是美国制造。iPhone 在加州设计、在中国制造，他说每年约 75% 的零件会换，只有约 25% 不变，所以稳定的那四分之一能上高度自动化的产线，其余靠手工装配。汽车厂可以花两年、2 亿美元上自动化，然后七年里大致造同一款车；iPhone 要想算得过账，得把自动化率从约 25% 提到约 90%，而那些专用机器连下一代 iPhone 都泛化不了。亚洲约 100 万人在组装 iPhone 或零部件，旁边约有 10,000 名制造工程师在定工艺。Arda 的目标是：把产品设计上传到云端，七天内设计或重构一条自动化产线，像一天之内重新培训一个工人那样容易；制造业觉得七天把设计变更推上产线已经算非常敏捷了。

他认为答案不是人形机器人：「两条机械臂加一个摄像头就够了」，有时一条臂加一个摄像头就够，因为装配线是没有新情况的封闭世界，不像去打扫一间没见过的卧室。现有机械臂已经能造这里约 90% 的产品，卡住的是经济性：这些资本设备每年都要重新设计、重建。传统自动化单元里一半工作在校准，感知和 RL 可以把精度要求放松，但封闭世界仍要求 99% 或 99.9% 的时候都把器件装对。Arda 第一年和国防制造商 Androl 一起做软件与 AI 平台：1 月拿到他们的系统访问权，4 月有了第一个用户，把一个 actuator 的虚拟构建从几天缩到 90 分钟，之后铺到所有产品部门，从无人机到导弹，并在做 Fury 协同作战飞机。Arda 不自己训模型；前沿模型当 orchestrator，调度 motion planning 和检查 CAD 的工具，团队得先给制造工程师造出相当于 IDE 和 Git 的东西，agent 才加得上价值。

他走到这里的路径是：2005 年从 Stanford AI lab 的博士退学，当时认定神经网络成不了；在 Palantir 十年；花一年半试机器人创业失败；再到 OpenAI 机器人团队，用一只 shadow hand 花两年学会解魔方，之后他说自己「再也不信机器人手了」。他把功劳给 Palantir 的 forward deployed engineering：没有客户准备用，就不做这个功能；像 Sarah Adams 这样的单个重度用户撑了产品约一年；这套打法只有在把 FDE 看到的东西产品化、而不是停在咨询时才有杠杆。谈机器人时，他猜 Astra、Fable 这类模型管用，是因为它们靠「预测真实世界数据里最后几个显著比特」学到了好表征，不是因为灌了大量机器人数据；它们慢、又不能微调，给专门做微调的公司留了空间。结尾他借 Dario 的「数据中心里的一国天才」说：让智能走出数据中心的是感知和行动，未来十年会是各种形态机器人的大量涌现，而不只是人形；以光速扩散的文明也解释了费米悖论：「我们只会在它们到达前一小会儿探测到。又或许，我们就是那道波。」这里的数字和拼写都按对话原样照录，feed 没有独立核实。
