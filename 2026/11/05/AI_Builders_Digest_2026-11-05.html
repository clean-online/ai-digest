Aaron Levie CEO, Box

Box CEO Aaron Levie opens the window at 22:34 Pacific on 11-02, the only post that falls on the previous Pacific day. His is the longest post in the window and the most sustained argument here and the only one in the X window with an explicit before-and-after productivity baseline: a few years ago, even in a capability-aligned field like coding, the best gain was maybe 30-50%, because models made mistakes, context windows were too limited for long running work and interfaces required highly synchronous interaction. Now models do far longer tasks with limited human interaction, harnesses support agentic work, and systems support many agents at once, so the upper limit has gone from 30-50% to likely 5-10X depending on the field. He says living at the frontier versus not paying attention can now be an order of magnitude apart, and that the gap will widen with RSI. These are his estimates, not measurements.

Ep 96: Nebius CTO on Racing the Hyperscalers Before AWS Wakes Up

Unsupervised Learning publishes Ep 96, Nebius CTO on Racing the Hyperscalers Before AWS Wakes Up, at 2026-11-02T13:54:35Z, Pacific 11-02 05:54:35. The central claim is that software is the moat, not access to GPUs. The same platform has to serve a customer consuming tens of thousands of GPUs in one interconnected cluster and a self-service customer running one GPU for half an hour. He rejects the term Neo Clouds: five or seven companies at the start, closer to three to 500 now by his own rough count, and says the next competition is AWS and Google Cloud. Two self-reported numbers carry no baseline: revenue is like, what, I think 1% of AWS nowadays, and overall demand in the next three years will increase tenfold, hundredfold maybe.

On why the hyperscalers did not take the whole market, he gives two reasons. They constantly juggle internal consumption against what they can rent out, and the world is in a chip shortage: if NVIDIA could have produced 10 x the chips, the whole market would have been shaped differently, because at their scale this is a relatively small business. The same 10 x line opens the cold open and is repeated inside the interview; it is one claim stated twice, not two sources. He adds that public AWS prices are not the real prices for proper deals.

Customer differences, he says, are about maturity rather than scale or workload. A newly founded lab may sign a billion dollar contract and care only about GPU, storage, performance and availability, not even where the GPUs sit. Enterprise contracts are smaller but come with certifications, compliance, security posture checks and requests to see source code. He says the team underestimated storage: clients now routinely pull 500 to 700 gigabytes per second from a shared file system of around three petabytes connected to a thousand servers or 8,000 GPUs, and 30 megawatts, a serious facility five years ago, is sometimes not enough for a single client.

On disaggregated inference, the line is everyone's talking about that and no one's doing that properly. He says there are promising prototype results and some disaggregated setups in production for the most demanding customers, but it is hard to operate and hard to give proper SLAs, so it is not the default for every workload. On inference optimization he is blunter: as an industry, we didn't optimize shit, and running open-weight models as published gives underwhelming results. A summer acquisition was made for talent in model optimization, model compression and speculative decoding, roughly doubling that team; specialized groups now work on kernel optimization and compression.

A complete management position comes near the end. Usage is not restricted and the budget is significant, but policy says whatever you did with an agent is still your result, your pull request, your code and your on-call shift; work created by a fully autonomous agent is marked as such, and autonomous agents will not touch core services for a while. His answer on what changed his mind is software engineering: a year ago AI coding was total bullshit and is now everyday tooling, and he personally offloads cognitive load to local agents and harnesses. What he watches now is outcome optimization, with companies bidding per outcome rather than per request; agents sometimes run for minutes without issuing a backend request to an LLM because the workload has moved back to code and CPU. He says he has stopped caring about models being larger or smarter and sees no novel architecture today.
