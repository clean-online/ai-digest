Andrej Karpathy AI Researcher

Andrej Karpathy posts once, at 10:14 Pacific, and it is the largest post in the window on all three counts, 36,797 likes, 3,269 reposts and 1,497 replies, about 4.9 times the likes and 14 times the reposts of the next post. It also answers, from the person himself, a question the 11-02 issue had to leave open from the outside.

HackerTube, introduced as a weekend hack: he was annoyed with YouTube’s recommendations, so he had Claude filter down 43M comments over 20 years of HN to 6,454 videos that keep being recommended. The claim is a “*Significantly* better viewing experience” and the cost is “~2 hours with Opus 5.5”. Three design choices are named: no logins, with state kept in the local browser session; a thumbnail that is a representative frame from the middle of the video rather than the hyper-optimised upload; and the underlying comment analysis shipped as one zip file, so anyone can point their own Claude at it and ask for their own version. There is also “HackerTube Longs”.

The process is where the numbers are, and it separates what he did from what the agent did. He uses Claude Code, the desktop app for Mac, with Opus 5.5 on Medium effort as his default, talks only to Claude, and names three things he did by hand in the browser: create a Google Cloud API key to query YouTube for video metadata, boot a VPS box for serving, and buy the domain name. Once the box was up he gave Claude the IP and told it to update, secure, harden, deploy and optimise the code, and “it did the whole thing”. He closes on “Many awesome projects that would have normally taken weeks/months even can now be few-shotted for breakfast. What else are we not thinking of.”

Two things close here and two stay open. The 11-02 issue recorded Swyx reporting second-hand that Karpathy runs medium effort on Opus 5.5 on Hackertube, with nothing in the feed describing what that was; today he says it himself, with the model, the effort setting, the elapsed time, the input size and the output size attached. What is still missing is anything a reader can check against the product: the link in the post does not resolve here, there is no count of videos watched, and there is no comparison against YouTube’s own recommendations that anyone else can run.

Ep 95: Modal CEO on the Future of Inference, the Chaos of the GPU Market & Origins of the Sandbox Bet

Unsupervised Learning records this one with the CEO of Modal, published 2026-10-28T13:03:16Z, Pacific 10-28 06:03. It is Ep 95, and it is new to this series, which has carried Ep 86, 89, 91, 92, 93 and 94. The chair at the opening is Patrick Chase, a GP at Redpoint, standing in for Jacob Efron, who is on the episode and asks most of the questions. The guest is called Eric once and Erik once in the transcript and is not given a surname on air.

The figures are the spine. Modal was at roughly $2M run rate when its Series A deck went out and has crossed $1B run rate since, about 100x in three years. Sandboxes, launched in 2023, are now about a third of revenue and a standalone $300M run rate business, having grown “a thousand x almost” since 2025. LLM inference is also about a third of revenue, and the company is at 150 engineers. He also says “we’ve run 15x in the last twelve months” without saying what is being multiplied.

The sandbox origin story is an argument about asymmetry. The first prototype took Akshat a few days, he spent a little time on the SDK, and it did not work immediately; in 2025 it “kinda blew up because I think models got really good at writing code”, after which the system was rewritten five times. Asked for the next one, he says there is nothing with the same profile today and names four candidates instead: low latency, voice-to-voice and real-time video, “almost like building a CDN for inference”; training and reinforcement learning for mid-sized models; and full operating systems, Mac, Windows, Android and iPhone.

The GPU market is described from inside it. Capacity exists but “you just have to look a little further, accept higher prices, accept longer terms”, and Modal now works with 25 clouds and closes two or three compute deals a week. GTC was “like an open air drug market, but, like, GPUs”, with hotel lobbies and late-night WhatsApp groups, and one connection came through a sales VP’s cousin who was golfing with someone. The reason it stays that way is that compute is “not like a fungible contract”. Some of the neo clouds are “former crypto miners who haven’t really thought about… running a twenty four seven operation”. H100s are still the most common chip at Modal even as they ramp hard on Blackwell.

On neo clouds the position is two-sided. They are “fine business models”, because contracts are locked in and GPU lifetimes have been longer than expected so the residual value is real, but they are “at their core… financing vehicles” with high leverage and a high cost of capital, and the three successful hyperscalers all sat inside companies with solid cash flow and a public listing. The structural risk he names is the 30%: a GPU commitment needs only about 30% up front, so raising a billion dollars can secure three billion of GPUs, leaving “implicit leverage… and a dependence on subsequent fundraising”. If that funding pulls back, the neo labs become neo clouds, which he says Mistral already did.

The prediction is where he says his mind changed. Asked whether GPU rental prices are higher or lower in twelve months: higher. “I actually changed my mind on this… in the last forty eight hours” — he read the Anthropic S-1, had an agent summarise it, and found a company that is “basically… profitable” to a degree he had not anticipated, which removes any incentive to slow capacity acquisition, and he assumes the same at OpenAI. It is the sixth reading of that filing in this series after five on 10-28, the first from outside X, and the first attached to a stated change of position rather than to a valuation.

Inside his own company the agent numbers are the ones that travel. “Probably… 95% of the lines of code that gets merged as Modal is probably written by agents to some extent.” The adoption pattern inverted his expectation: he assumed juniors would adopt first and it is “almost the other way around”, the very senior engineers, because for them it is “like giving them… 10 interns” and they had been constrained by typing speed. He still says humans supervise heavily because infrastructure has “a lot of tail risks”, that software engineers are “pretty safe… for the next few years”, and that short term it will be harder for new grads “except for that, the very high side of the spectrum”. He would still encourage his own kids to study computer science.

Two operational consequences of agents being the user are named, and neither is about capability. Deprecations: agents are trained on old code, so “removing features is much harder for us” and they can no longer just pull something from the documentation and wait three months. And what he calls agent optimisation, which is “more like similar… to search engine optimization”: ask Claude Code which tool to use to deploy a model and see what it recommends. The consequence he draws is about incumbency — “I’ve heard people say, like, oh, I’m just gonna use React for everything because agents are really good at it” — and it is why being inside the training data is now “very, very valuable”. In passing he notes that Anthropic “a couple days ago… announced they’re moving off Git, I believe, or at least GitHub”.

On what lasts: he will not go into model training, he thinks post-training risks becoming “just a service business” unless it is turned into a product, he expects every successful company to end up multiproduct, and he dislikes the word agentic because “shouldn’t all software do that?” The customers named are Cognition, which uses inference and sandboxes for reinforcement learning, Ramp, which launched its internal coding agent as a standalone product, Suno, Runway, Decagon, DoorDash, computational biotech firms, and robotics companies including 1x. He also asks why voice assistants are “so bad in 2026” and answers with his own Google Home.

核心结论

计数器走到了它自己说过的那个数。Sottiaux 的「Day 28/ Banked 2/2」是本系列里第一条同时带日期编号和分数的推文，而 Day 28 落在两个独立推算都指向的那一天：从 Day 22（10-26）和 Day 24（10-28）按一天一号往下数，Day 28 是太平洋 11-01；从 Day 21 在太平洋 10-25 给出的七天往下数，也是同一天。两个算法都落在它发布的那天。八条带编号的推文里始终没有动的是宾语 —— 没有任何一个 Day 说过被数的是什么、被 banked 的是什么；而这条以 7,495 赞、233 次转发、1,342 条回复排在三项的第二。

Karpathy 本人、并且带着数字回答了 11-02 那个问题。HackerTube 是把 20 年 HN 上的 4,300 万条评论筛成 6,454 个视频，用 Opus 5.5 大约两小时，人工只做了三步 —— 一个 API key、一台 VPS、一个域名 —— 其余全部交出去，包括「更新、加固、防护、部署和优化」。它以 36,797 赞、3,269 次转发、1,497 条回复在三项上都排第一，赞数约为第二名的 4.9 倍。它还由跑它的那个人本人确认了 11-02 那期的转述：Opus 5.5、medium 档。

「Own your intelligence」现在有两个信源，而两者之间没有任何桥。Rauch 这一版有 2,251 赞、222 次转发、242 条回复，主张本地模型、开源，以及握住自己的 harness、数据、连接和运行时。11-02 那期 Arca 的买方用的是同一个想法，用来解释他为什么对 managed agents 没那么积极。两人互不引用，都没有任何测量，方向却一致 —— 所以本系列把它们记成两条，而不是记成一种趋势。

这个窗口里唯一的价格比拿到了最少的赞。Garry Tan 的 Particle-1，「在 agentic retrieval 上和大得多的模型一样好，价格是 1/23」，正是 10-30 那期缺的东西 —— 三位 builder 点名它时什么都没带上，而现在有了名字、任务和数字；它拿到的是 30 赞、3 次转发、14 条回复，22 条里赞数倒数第三，排在它前面的两条一条是裸链接、一条是引文。Amjad 那句关于补贴订阅的一句话警告拿到了 2,266。这个窗口奖励的是句子，不是证据。

播客是迟到的，但它是那份文件这么多读法里唯一推动了什么的一读。它发布于太平洋 10-28，比本期快照早将近四天，是本系列里第六次读到那份 Anthropic S-1，也是第一次来自 X 之外：Modal 的 CEO 在四十八小时内改了自己对 GPU 价格的判断，因为一份 agent 摘要让他看到一家「基本上……盈利」的公司，而这让放慢算力采购失去了理由。这是一个预测，它架在一个人对一份本系列从未见过的文件的阅读上。

这次覆盖没有缺口。22 条里有 21 条落在太平洋 11-01 之内，第 22 条在太平洋 10-31 23:39，比 11-02 那期收到的最后一条晚三十六分钟，所以两期之间没有丢东西。太平洋 10-29 与 10-30 仍然因为更早那次运行而永久缺失。
