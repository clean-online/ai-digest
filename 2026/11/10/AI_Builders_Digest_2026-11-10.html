The idea started in November 2024, when coding agents were earlier and the methodology was scattered across blog posts; he spent about nine to ten months building a proposal, got it through a committee, and first taught it in fall 2025, before Opus 4.5 and 4.6, with a Business Insider article about it last year. He took inspiration from Chip Hieatt, who taught a machine learning systems design class. This year autonomous coding agents picked up in a way not seen before, so the course was refreshed. His sharpest line is that most students are cooked and some of them are going to be super valuable; he calls it a spicy thing to say but says he genuinely believes it, and asks what happens to the rest of America. On the first day of class he tells students every company needs them but the floor is much higher: being an L3 in Google’s leveling is not sufficient, they generally have to be near senior level going in. The class is not for beginners and does not replace traditional CS; algorithms classes are prerequisites.

Last year was a broad tour of the ISDLC, with a coding agent for code review, one for spec design, one for SRE, and nine pedagogical assignments plus an open-ended capstone that he thinks many students could have one-shotted. This year the principles are solidifying: careful context engineering, and design and architecture for complex tasks. He surveyed the class on Tuesday and not a single person really types by hand anymore, all sophomores and juniors at Stanford. The nine assignments are replaced by contributions to open source partners he spent most of the summer recruiting, where maintainers review student code and pass on mental models of good software. Beyond the Claude, Cursor and Cognition licenses the university already provides, he accrued extra credits from Factory, Cognition and OpenAI so students are not bottlenecked by a $20 a month subscription. The ten weeks run from harness internals and building a coding agent from scratch in week one, through context engineering, tool design and MCP in week two (“I’m still pro MCP”), skills in week three, agent config files, hooks and sub-agents in week four, agent-ready code bases in week five, agentic code review in week six, security in week seven, background agents in week eight, team-level infrastructure in week nine, to the software factory in week ten. His blog post argues that an agentic loop that talks to you and edits your file needs no more than two hundred lines and no more than Stanford 106B, introductory algorithms; no sub-agents, no MCP.

Code review became the new bottleneck: once generation velocity is high you could in theory put up 30 plus PRs a day, but if you insert yourself as the gate you become the choke point of that velocity. He wants the middle ground, a class of problems you can auto-approve some percentage of the time once thoughtful design has gone into agent readiness and verifiability, and asks whether careful review still means line-by-line or something else, with the PR description effectively serving as the current spec. He calls code review the final boss, alongside intent, as the two choke points in a fully automated system, and points to posts from Amplitude, Cloudflare, Augment and Ankit of Aviator on killing the code review. At Monaco they follow “buy to learn and build to keep” (he says it the other way round first and corrects himself), built their own background agent platform Monocoder on top of Coder and a Slack bot called Hal that is part AI SRE, part customer success, part QA engineer, and adopted Devin for code review only after an organically spreading internal recommendation, having earlier done a statistically driven PR analysis across CodeRabbit, Graphite and Ellipsis and picked no vendor.

On security his objection is not that models write buggier code. He says models will on average have fewer bugs, but they introduce bugs you would not make as a human, and the deeper problem is that you do not even know what you are putting out because you are no longer reviewing everything. His rule is that not all parts of a code base are created equal: data, billing and auth are don’t-write-code zones, everything else go nuts, and if you do not know the difference between authn and authz or have never looked at AWS IAM, learn it, because once you have the language you can prompt for it and without the language you are stuck. He is unconvinced by the belief that models will fix all security, asking what if they introduce new and different bugs, and mentions a proposal attributed to Malta of Vercel to port highly dependent software written in C and C++ to Rust for about $6 million, alongside Daybreak, Glasswing and Pacing the Frontier from the model labs. On dependencies he notes a package with 100,000 users worldwide will have bugs found by someone, while your own custom dependency will be discovered slowly.
