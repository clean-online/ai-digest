The through-line is a three-step account of the field: mapping with human cell atlases, then prediction with world models, and now steering the next experiment. Theis calls a virtual cell a world model over transcriptome or other omics space, not F of X but F of X and the action A, predicting the next state. He thinks equating a virtual cell with perturbation prediction is a bit too simplistic; the deeper question is whether you can predict how a cell behaves under all kinds of perturbations, which he calls maybe even a holy grail of computational biology. Understanding, in his framing, means predicting a system’s output behaviour given different inputs.

The most portable idea is a discipline about baselines. His lab’s 2019 scGen did linear shifts in a latent space, and a paper of his was plainly titled “Deep Learning is not needed for cell type prediction”; within one organ, logistic regression or a tool like CellTypist still does the job, and it only gets subtler across organs. He points at the Arc Institute Virtual Cell Challenge, whose fixed metrics were not single-cell specific and could be exploited, and at a Nature paper by Bo Wang and collaborators arguing the metric was the problem. His line to students: “I know you wanna do the most fancy deep learning thing, but please, please, you know, have the baseline.” For drugs the hardest baseline is simply better than the mean, and the data are very noisy.

On scale, the numbers are his own characterisations. A Pars Bio “giga lab” measured ten million transcriptomes in a single experiment; the perturbation space has widened from control versus disease to a hundred cell lines with full CRISPR knockouts or batteries of a thousand drugs. His contrastive-learning paper scConcert was compared with Geneformer up to maybe thirty million cells, and on Hugging Face they now have a three hundred and fifty million cell model going up to maybe a hundred million parameters, with scaling behaviour starting to emerge, and sixteen species in the latent space. Cell flow, a flow-matching generative model along the action, was accepted at Nature Methods and depends heavily on a good latent space. He says the literature shows strong a priori inductive biases cost predictive power, cites AlphaFold learning rotational symmetry from more data, and concludes that at some point data beats human-made additional concepts; but in the current perturbation-space data regime hybrid models are still the way to go.

On the business side, a host mentions Biohub announcing a one point eight billion dollar collaboration with the DOE, NIH, Meta and Google to collect and open source multi-scale biological data for a new generation of virtual cells. Theis is blunt about platform companies that say they will decode biology without saying what they will do with it: many pivot to “We make a drug” because that is what investors understand. He puts the sweet spot in coupling modelling with unique data generation, citing a Cancer Organoid Perturbation Atlas consortium where pharma buys early access to the datasets, and says the moat is increasingly the specific data alongside the virtual cell model. On Helmholtz Munich he describes a Computational Health Center with maybe forty-five group leaders and professors and five hundred to six hundred people, no undergraduate teaching, only PhD students and postdocs, and a longer-term vision of a virtual human. On Germany he says the EU creates as many startups as the US but drops off at the scaling stage, where big-ticket series A and B money is missing.

The episode also describes the Munich School for Data Science, running about eight years, where two PIs, one methods-oriented and one domain-oriented, jointly supervise a student in what he calls a Y model, “Y plus Y equals H”, with the student attending two lab meetings. He mentions NVIDIA hiring two of his people in one month. He calls the ultimate vision “we want to RL everything” but immediately adds “It’s a bit out there” and says at the moment it is more like active learning.
