No Priors
The Changing Physics of Cybersecurity and Drug Development with OpenAI President Greg Brockman

The Takeaway — The line that will travel is the money one: security spend should “vastly outstrip” software spend, because software is getting cheap enough to stop being the thing you are buying. Behind it sit a window, a programme and one internal exercise, and only the internal exercise comes with a number attached.

The security argument, in his own words. “The physics of cybersecurity are changing”, and the world is in “what we call the defender’s window”: peak frontier capabilities can already help defenders, they are “not broadly diffused yet”, and the capabilities that are broadly diffused “are ratcheting” from one model release to the next across providers. The evidence he offers is a sentence: “in South Korea that there were some high profile hacks of banks that were driven by different models” — no bank, no date, no number, no attribution beyond the country. The mechanism offered to defenders is the Daybreak programme, to “responsibly give access through our Daybreak program to defenders to be able to use our peak intelligence or to use very capable models to find and fix vulnerabilities in their systems”, alongside a “billion dollar commitment” to get frontline defenders access to the models, and one named partner, CrowdStrike.

This moves something the series has been carrying. The 10-20 issue recorded an offer of “Daybreak Blue access”, glossed only as a “cyber permissive setup for defense”, and asked what it grants and who can get it. Both halves now have an answer of sorts: access to capable models for finding and fixing vulnerabilities, for defenders. Neither source says what Blue adds to the programme, or what “cyber permissive” means, so that part stays open.

The one number in the security passage is also two numbers. He says “we took about 25 of our production engineers and said, of your projects are on hold. You are just securing now”, and a host later paraphrases the same move as “25% of the engineers”. Both are printed here and not reconciled, because the transcript supports either reading. What the exercise produced is not quantified: the model was pointed at all of their infrastructure, “radiating a bunch of things”, they “found a number of issues, we fixed it”, and “at some point we saturated the issues that this model was capable of finding” — no count, no duration, no list, and the point he draws is that it will have to be repeated with the next model. The case for why now is about debt: the internet “was really built for resilience, not necessarily for security”, security got “bolted on” afterwards, and the tools that would have paid it down, formal verification among them, were painful and niche, so “no one does it for anything significant”.

Drug development is the second half, and it is where the figures are weakest. He calls drug discovery “the most clear and obvious area” to revolutionise and gives first principles rather than evidence as the reason: humans are well suited to natural language and “incredibly bad at biology”, while a neural net has no reason to be better at one than the other. The costs come from a host, as transcribed: fifteen years and $1,500,000,000, of which “15,000,000 or so” is preclinical and “1,400,000,000 plus” is the clinical trial, with two or three years against another twelve. Those three figures do not add up — 15M and 1.4B leave the total short of 1.5B — and this issue prints them as transcribed rather than correcting them. His own bottleneck is data, and he says it is unresolved: “do we have the data? Do we have the ability to get the data?” The bottleneck he does name is not biological at all: regulatory filings, where “it takes like a year to write up your whole sort of case for a clinical trial”. On how much of it arrives, he offers “we will see, like, 80% of what is possible, come to fruition very quickly”, with no baseline and no date.

On mathematics he gives the episode’s most concrete research number: “we published about almost 400 very important open problems that are now resolved”, with the mathematical community going through them “to look for insights” and domain experts reporting “surprising ways of applying ideas”. He states the ceiling himself — “we’re not yet coming up with whole, brand new theories” — and the distinction he draws is with the four colour theorem, where “we just had the machine check a bunch of cases, like there’s no beauty in that”, so “we’re into a different phase of machine discovery”. No paper, no list and no problem is named, so the 400 cannot be checked from here.

On product, four things. The scale figure arrives in two forms inside one conversation: a host says GPT-six was rolled out “to over a billion people”, and he says OpenAI has “1,200,000,000 consumers on ChatGPT”; both are printed rather than averaged. On interface, “the best UI is no UI”, with the enterprise difference located in admins, controls and toggles, and the tension he names himself: if there is no interface, “what does it mean for there to be functionality that’s missing and how do you even surface it”. On generated interface, “intelligent UI”, where the model builds the thing you need at the moment you need it, his two examples being flight booking and a one-off app for assembling a bicycle. And on agents, DOTS: “a proactive agent, it’s always on, always working, you stop working, it keeps working”, which watches his inbox, Slack, calendar and projects, wakes him with a list of things he said he would do, notices scheduling conflicts he never asked about, runs as “an Astra level model” with “its own cloud computer”, and is, in his framing, “a change to the form factor, not the capability”. He runs one, and its icon is a little owl.

Four provenance notes. The episode is dated 2026-10-15T10:00Z and this feed’s snapshot is 2026-10-26T06:55Z, so it arrives eleven days old and nothing in it is new by calendar. The url field gives the No Priors channel rather than a link to this episode, which is why the link below goes to the channel. The transcript is machine-generated and the errors touch proper nouns — “Greg Brockmiff”, “GPD four”, “OpenNAR”, “GPT-six” — so every model and product name above is printed as transcribed. And on speakers, the transcript labels them only Speaker 1, 2 and 3; the introduction gives “Elad and I”, and his thanks comes back as “Thanks, Regina”, so no host name is filled in here.

One name to reconcile across the series. Dots, spelled that way, was the subject of Dan Shipper’s long first-hand review in the 10-09 issue — permissions that fail even after approval, and a claim of rough feature parity with Muse, Instinct and Grok bots — and that issue did not say who makes it. 核心结论 —— 会被传开的是那句关于钱的话：安全支出应当「远远超过」软件支出，因为软件正在便宜到不再是你要买的那个东西。它后面站着的是一个窗口、一个计划，和一次内部演练，而三者里只有那次内部演练带着数字。
