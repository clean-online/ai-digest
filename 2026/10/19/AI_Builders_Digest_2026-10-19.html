The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck
What Happens When Billions of AI Agents Hit Your Database? (Andy Pavlo)

Andy Pavlo taught a generation of engineers about databases at Carnegie Mellon and put the whole curriculum online for free; roughly two months before this recording he left to found ClickHouse Labs inside ClickHouse. He gives two reasons for leaving academia, and the second one is the one that lands: US research funding “is not what it used to be”, and there is a real question now about what a PhD student is for when an agent can do a lot of the work. His answer to the title question is that nobody knows yet, and he keeps saying so: “I gotta be honest, I don’t know the answer.” What he does open with is a failure rate: “Every month, there’s a story where someone lost their production database because the agent deleted something.”

On the number that has been circulating, he corrects the object. The host cites the Neon figure of agents going from 30% to 80%; Pavlo says the metric was about branches, meaning copy-on-write snapshots of a real database that a human or an agent can change without touching production, and that this is mostly development and testing activity, PR runs and GitHub Actions, rather than agents creating databases from scratch. For context inside this series: the 10-10 issue recorded Turck’s version of that 80%, noting the chapter heading itself ended in a question mark, and here the guest narrows what it counts. On vector search he is blunt: the databases existed since about 2016, RAG gave them their moment, and “at the end of the day, it’s just an index”, which every vendor shipped within a year.

His prescription is unglamorous and specific. Think of agents as toddlers and put up guardrails, because babysitting them with approve-approve-approve does not scale to hundreds at once. Use branching so changes land on a snapshot and not on production. Set permissions so they cannot drop tables, and also so they cannot read what they should not read. Do not hand the agent the one password the whole company reuses. None of this is new, in his telling, and that is the complaint: “for years, we’ve had the capabilities to make sure people don’t do things they shouldn’t be doing”, and people are learning the hard way because everyone wants to reinvent the wheel. Asked whether agents behave differently from humans at the API level, he says he has seen nothing to suggest it, apart from running around the clock.

On volume he gives a range and attaches his own uncertainty to it: potentially a 10 to 100x increase in queries hitting databases, and “I don’t think we’re at that level yet”, though he says he is hearing it from friends in industry and seeing it at ClickHouse. His reasoning is a four-era story: mainframes in the seventies and eighties when only people at work touched a database, the web in the nineties and two-thousands, mobile after that, and now every person potentially having hundreds of agents running all night. The part he flags as the real unknown is sophistication rather than volume, whether agent SQL is shallower than a BI tool doing complex joins across tables, and he says twice that he does not know yet. Agents generating their own telemetry is a separate storage problem on top.

The sharpest thing in the episode is about defaults. Agents almost always recommend Postgres, he says, unless you say you are running embedded, which gets you DuckDB or SQLite, or that you only care about key-value, which gets you RocksDB. Two database companies he declines to name asked him how to get their database recommended first over Postgres; he told them that is not research, to go talk to the people building the models, and calls it “basically like SEO”. He also has the problem from the other side: when he asks about databases, models cite him back to himself, and once he excludes his own name and Carnegie Mellon they cite another university course that is using his slides.

On text-to-SQL he is skeptical of academic benchmarks like Spider because the models are trained on them. The numbers he gives are second-hand and he says so: someone at a very large bank told him yesterday that an off-the-shelf implementation was about 60% accurate, and that after building a richer semantic layer over multiple years and millions of dollars they reached about 99.5%. His reading is that it is therefore viable if you invest, and that a random person downloading a tool today will not get that. On MCP he is pragmatic rather than excited: he has no problem with it, it is basically a REST interface rather than a magic bullet, it is inefficient because it sends human-readable text over the network where binary protocols would do better, and it is good enough and everyone uses it. Guardrails are the same whether the request arrives as SQL or over MCP.

On whether agents can build databases: a year ago they could not finish his CMU course project; after “Opus 4” they could do all the assignments with very little prompting, helped by the fact that the projects are open source. He repeats the old adage that you build the first 90% in three years and the remaining 10% takes the next seven, and says you can vibe code an entire database system with enough tokens and guidance. On his dbdb.io project, which pulls every open source database’s latest commits nightly and checks whether they are co-signed by Claude or Codex, he puts the figure at over 60%, with the caveat he supplies himself that people can turn that off. On self-driving: LLMs get you 80 to 85% of the way in about fifteen minutes where his old models took hours to train, and what surprised him was how many companies ran out-of-the-box cloud configs believing the cloud was tuning it for them (“no, they’re not”). Index-picking and knob-tuning “ships out”; data modeling still needs someone.

The closing positions are the ones worth keeping. He assumed ClickHouse was vaporware in 2016 because it looked too good to be true, and now calls its architecture not novel by 2026 standards, with the lineage running through Snowflake and VectorWise. On convergence, Databricks buying Neon and Snowflake buying Crunchy, his conjecture, which he labels biased and says he cannot prove, is that analytics-first and then adding OLTP is the better order; the blocker on one unified system has always been organizational, because each team wants the best system for its own workload. Vector databases are not going away, and he names Turbo Puffer as the one he knows is doing very well. Graph databases are, in his words, a horrible idea, and he has a standing Hacker News bet that would make him wear an “I love graph databases” shirt if the graph market passes relational by 2030, which he says is not happening with four years to go. On GPU databases, Nvidia has absorbed several struggling ones, Microsoft offers GPU-accelerated analytics, and another major data company he cannot name looked at the economics and decided against it, with his own interest note attached: “NVIDIA is obviously pushing this because it’ll sell more GPUs.”

His frame on whether the database market has gone stagnant is the line that generalises best. There was an explosion in the seventies and eighties, consolidation around Oracle, IBM and Teradata in the nineties, another explosion in the two-thousands and twenty-tens, and “relative to AI, then everything looks stagnant”. Foundations do not get thrown away: “in the same way... you wouldn’t come up with a new notion of arithmetic or mathematics to replace one plus one equals two.” SQL may end up like assembly, something nobody writes by hand any more, because text-to-SQL works well enough. The version he finds genuinely open is hyper-specialization, vibe coding a bespoke data system per application, and whether that can be sustained.

Andy Pavlo 在卡内基梅隆教了一代工程师数据库，并把整套课程免费放到了网上；在这期录制的大约两个月前，他离开学校，在 ClickHouse 内部创办了 ClickHouse Labs。他给了两个离开学术界的理由，第二个才是真正有分量的那个：美国的研究经费“已经不是过去那个样子了”，而当 agent 能干掉很多工作时，“博士生到底是来做什么的”成了一个真问题。他对标题里那个问题的回答是：现在没人知道，而且他反复这么说：“老实说，我不知道答案。”他开口给出的是一个故障率：“每个月都有一则新闻，说某人丢了生产库，因为 agent 删了不该删的东西。”

关于那个一直在流传的数字，他纠正的是对象。主持人引用的是 Neon 那个从 30% 到 80% 的说法；Pavlo 说那个指标讲的是 branch，也就是对一个真实数据库做写时复制的快照，人或 agent 可以在上面改动而不碰到生产库，而这件事主要发生在开发和测试环节（跑 PR、跑 GitHub Actions），不是 agent 从零开始建库。本系列内部的对照是：10-10 那期记录过 Turck 转述的 80%，并注明那章标题本身以问号结尾；而这里，嘉宾把它计的东西收窄了。至于向量检索，他说得很直接：那些数据库大约 2016 年就在了，RAG 给了它们出场时机，而“说到底它只是一个索引”，一年之内每家厂商都做出来了。

他开的方子不花哨，而且很具体。把 agent 当成幼儿，装上围栏，因为坐在旁边一路点“同意、同意、同意”在几百个 agent 同时干活时是扩不了规模的。用 branch，让改动落在快照上而不是生产库上。设权限，让它们既不能删表，也不能读不该读的东西。不要把全公司共用的那个密码交给 agent。在他看来这些都不新，而这正是他的抱怨所在：“多年来我们一直有能力阻止人们做不该做的事”，而大家非要重造轮子，于是只能吃一堑长一智。被问到 agent 在 API 层面的行为是否与人类不同，他说除了二十四小时不停之外，没有见过任何证据。

关于量，他给了一个区间，并把自己的不确定挂在了上面：打到数据库上的查询量可能有 10 到 100 倍的增长，而“我觉得我们还没到那个量级”，不过他说行业里的朋友在反馈这件事，他在 ClickHouse 也看到了。他的推理是一条四个时代的线：七八十年代的大型机，只有上班的人会碰数据库；九十年代到两千年代的互联网；再之后是手机；现在是每个人可能都有几百个 agent 通宵在跑。他真正标记为未知的不是量，而是复杂程度：agent 写出来的 SQL 会不会比一个做多表复杂 join 的 BI 工具更浅，这一点他连说两次“我还不知道”。另外 agent 自己产生的遥测数据是叠在上面的另一个存储问题。

这期里最锋利的东西是关于默认选项。他说 agent 几乎总是推荐 Postgres，除非你说要跑在嵌入式设备上（那就给你 DuckDB 或 SQLite），或者只关心键值（那就给你 RocksDB）。有两家数据库公司问他怎么让自己的数据库排在 Postgres 前面被推荐，他拒绝点名，说这不是研究，让他们去找做模型的人，并把它称为“基本上就是 SEO”。他也有反过来的那个麻烦：当他问数据库的问题时，模型会把他自己引回给他自己，而等他把自己的名字和卡内基梅隆都排除掉，模型就去引用另一所大学的课程，而那门课用的还是他的课件。

关于 text-to-SQL，他对 Spider 这类学术基准持怀疑态度，因为模型是在它们上面训练出来的。他给的数字是二手的，而他自己也说明了：一家非常大的银行的某个人昨天告诉他，直接拿现成方案落地大约是 60% 的准确率，而在花了几年的时间、数百万美元去做一个更丰富的语义层之后，他们做到了大约 99.5%。他的读法是：只要肯投入，这条路是可行的；而今天随便一个人下载一个工具，拿不到这个水平。关于 MCP，他是务实而不是兴奋：他没意见，它基本上就是一个 REST 接口，不是什么万能药，效率也不高，因为它在网络上发的是人类可读的文本，而二进制协议本来可以做得更好；但它够用了，而且大家都在用。无论请求是以 SQL 进来还是走 MCP 进来，围栏是同一套。

关于 agent 能不能造数据库：一年前它们做不完他在 CMU 的课程项目；“Opus 4” 之后，它们几乎不需要什么提示就能做完所有作业，而这些项目是开源的，训练数据充足。他复述了那句老话：前 90% 你用三年做出来，剩下 10% 再花七年；他说只要有足够的 token 和引导，你确实可以 vibe 出一整套数据库系统。他的 dbdb.io 项目每天夜里把所有开源数据库的最新 commit 拉下来，检查是否由 Claude 或 Codex 共同署名，他给出的比例是超过 60%，而他自己补上了前提：有人会把这个功能关掉。关于自驾驶：LLM 大约十五分钟就能把你带到 80% 到 85%，而他以前的模型要训练好几个小时；真正让他意外的是，有多少家公司拿着云上开箱即用的配置在跑，还以为云厂商在替他们调优（“不，他们没有”）。挑索引、调参数这件事“会退出历史舞台”；数据建模仍然需要有人来做。

结尾那几个立场最值得留下。他说自己在 2016 年以为 ClickHouse 只是个画出来的假产品（他用的词是 vaporware），因为它好得不太真实，而现在他认为它的架构“以 2026 年的标准来看并不新鲜”，谱系经由 Snowflake 和 VectorWise。关于融合（Databricks 收购 Neon、Snowflake 收购 Crunchy），他的猜测是先做分析、再加 OLTP 是更好的顺序，而这个猜测他自己标注了立场偏差，也说无法证明；统一系统一直卡住的原因从来是组织层面的，因为每一方都想为自己的负载拿到最好的那个系统。向量数据库不会消失，他点名 Turbo Puffer 是他知道的做得非常好的一家。图数据库，用他的话说，是个糟糕的主意，他在 Hacker News 上还有一个悬着的赌注：如果到 2030 年图数据库市场规模超过关系型数据库，他就穿上印着“我爱图数据库”的衬衫；他说还剩四年，这件事不会发生。关于 GPU 数据库：NVIDIA 已经收掉了好几家苦苦支撑的公司，微软提供 GPU 加速的分析服务，而另一家他不能点名的大数据公司算过这笔经济账之后决定不做，他自己还附上了利益说明：“NVIDIA 推这个显然是因为能多卖 GPU。”

他对“数据库市场是不是停滞了”给出的框架，是这里面最能推广的一句。七八十年代有一次爆发，九十年代围绕 Oracle、IBM、Teradata 完成整合，两千年代和二零一零年代又爆发一次，而“跟 AI 比起来，那一切都显得停滞”。地基是不会被扔掉的：“同样地，你不会为了取代一加一等于二，去发明一套新的算术或数学。”SQL 也许会变成汇编那样的东西，没人再手写它，因为 text-to-SQL 已经足够好用。他真正认为还开着的那条路是极度专用化，为每个应用 vibe 出一套定制的数据系统，以及这件事能不能长期维持。

The Takeaway

The through-line is agents reaching outside their owner, and the same issue carries both the reach and the guardrail.
