1 Episode
Training Data
David Silver and Lasse Espeholt of Ineffable Intelligence: A Superlearner for the Age of Experience

David Silver, who led AlphaGo, and Lasse Espeholt, who did the first work on scaling reinforcement learning, are two of the co-founders of Ineffable Intelligence, and this is the first time either has appeared in this feed. Silver gives his reason for leaving DeepMind as a bet rather than a grievance: the path to superintelligence he believes in does not pass through LLM-based products as an intermediate step, and he did not want to be “the 100,001st person” on the approach that tens or hundreds of thousands of researchers are already pursuing. The stated mission is a super learner and first contact with superintelligence.

The definition is given on two axes and it is the most precise thing in the episode. Depth: something that starts from knowing nothing at all and reaches discoveries beyond what humans know. Breadth: something you can drop into any stream of experience — simulated, digital or physical — and it keeps learning. He is explicit that today's LLMs are not this, on two grounds: the breakthroughs still require a great deal of human in the loop, and it is not at all clear that language-based approaches are the right vehicle for knowledge that comes from physical experience.

The bet is stated as a deliberate sacrifice. Ineffable is giving up human data — the internet of knowledge everyone else builds on — because he argues you cannot reach autonomous discovery while relying on it, and he describes the LLM labs as building semi-autonomous learning algorithms with thousands of people inside the loop. The precedent he cites is his own: going from AlphaGo to AlphaZero took a few hours to beat two years of human-in-the-loop work, four hours in chess. He also says the position is pragmatic rather than religious: weak, environment-agnostic priors are fine, trillion-item priors are what they are avoiding.

Instead of hand-crafted task sets they build what they call crucibles: environments with enough emergent complexity that discovery becomes possible, whether from physics engines running millions of interacting objects or from millions of interacting agents. The design principle is explicitly not to write a reward that encodes what they mean by science or mathematics; it is to give a simple objective inside a complex environment and let complexity emerge, the way evolution optimises fitness and gets life. His own illustration is the cleanest: Go is a 19 by 19 grid, and as a single-agent game it would be the most boring game imaginable, but adding one opponent produces an endless learning curve.

Two numbers appear, and one gap. Silver says they could probably run experiments using 100,000 times more compute than OpenAI's hide-and-seek work, and calls compute a huge driver but not the only one. The gap is that the cluster is never sized — Espeholt says only that they have secured a very large cluster, and no figure for it or for funding appears anywhere in the transcript. The one headcount given is 20 people hired, kept small deliberately to hold compute per person high.

On simulation he makes the strongest version of the argument that is available: if superintelligence can be built in simulation it will happen ten or a hundred times faster and it is the safest place to build it, because simulation parallelises infinitely and is perfectly reproducible. His case that it is possible is structural — a Turing-complete simulation can express everything meant by computation, a representation of money can express economics, a channel between agents can express language — and he concedes the ceiling: quantum computation is his own example of something not expressible, and he agrees with Rich Sutton that you should not expect what is learned in simulation to transfer to the real world.

Asked for dates, he says they will have extremely strong evidence they are on the right track within a year, and that true superintelligence — his definition being something that discovers things far beyond human reach across science, mathematics, technology and languages — will take three years, accelerated from the three to five he originally held, because of what coding agents can do. On monitoring he says the language-based approach everyone relies on is reaching its limits: monitors face a million words of reasoning, or active subterfuge where a system thinks one way and acts another, so he proposes shadow actions — let the agent act where you can inspect it, without letting the action land. Their commitment is to gate anything in the real world on evidence from this science of superintelligence. None of it is a measurement; all of it is a forecast or a design principle.

David Silver 主导过 AlphaGo，Lasse Espeholt 做过最早把强化学习规模化起来的工作，两人都是 Ineffable Intelligence 的联合创始人，这也是他们第一次出现在这个 feed 里。Silver 给出的离开 DeepMind 的理由是一个赌注，不是怨气：他相信的通向超级智能的路径并不以基于 LLM 的产品作为中间站，而他不想成为已经有数万乃至数十万研究者在走的这条路上的“第 100,001 个人”。公司对外说的使命是做出一个 super learner，并与超级智能完成第一次接触。

这个定义是沿着两个轴给出的，也是这一集里最精确的东西。深度：从一无所知开始，一直走到超出人类已知范围的发现。广度：可以把它丢进任何经验流 —— 模拟的、数字的、物理的 —— 它都能继续学。他明确说今天的 LLM 不是这个东西，理由有两条：那些突破仍然需要大量人在环里，而且基于语言的方法到底是不是承载来自物理经验的知识的正确载体，完全不清楚。

这个赌注被表述成一次主动的牺牲。Ineffable 放弃了人类数据 —— 也就是其他所有人都在其上构建的那个知识互联网 —— 因为他的论证是：只要还依赖它，就到不了自主发现；他把那些 LLM 实验室描述成正在构建半自主的学习算法，环里有成千上万的人。他引用的先例是他自己的：从 AlphaGo 到 AlphaZero，用了几个小时就超过了人在环里做了两年的成果，国际象棋是四个小时。他还说这个立场是务实的而不是原教旨的：弱的、与环境无关的先验可以接受，他们要避开的是万亿条那种级别的先验。

他们不做手工拼出来的任务集，而是构建所谓 crucibles（熔炉）：具有足够涌现复杂度的环境，让发现这件事成为可能 —— 复杂性可以来自跑着数百万个交互对象的物理引擎，也可以来自数百万个互相交互的 agent。设计原则明确不是去写一个把“科学”或“数学”编码进去的奖励函数，而是在一个复杂环境里给一个简单的目标，让复杂性自己涌现出来，就像进化只优化适应度却得到了整个生命。他自己的例子最干净：围棋是一个 19 乘 19 的网格，作为单人游戏它会是你所能想象的最无聊的游戏，但只要加进一个对手，就出现了没有尽头的学习曲线。

这一集里有两个数字，还有一处空缺。Silver 说他们大概能跑比 OpenAI 当年 hide-and-seek 那类工作多用 100,000 倍算力的实验，并称算力是巨大的驱动因素，但不是唯一的。空缺在于集群的规模始终没有给出 —— Espeholt 只说他们拿到了一个很大的集群，整个文稿里没有任何关于它、或者关于融资的数字。唯一给出的人数是 20 人，刻意保持小规模，为的是让人均算力维持在高位。

关于模拟，他给出了这个论证最强的版本：如果超级智能可以在模拟中被造出来，那它会快十倍到一百倍，而且模拟是最安全的建造场所 —— 因为模拟可以无限并行，并且完全可复现。他论证“这是可能的”时用的是结构性理由 —— 一个图灵完备的模拟可以表达计算的全部含义，一种货币的表示可以表达经济学，agent 之间的一条通道可以表达语言 —— 而他也承认天花板：量子计算就是他自己举的、无法在模拟中表达的例子；并且他同意 Rich Sutton 的说法 —— 不该指望在模拟里学到的东西能迁移到真实世界。

被问到时间点，他说一年之内会拿到他们走在正确道路上的极强证据；而真正的超级智能 —— 他的定义是能在科学、数学、技术、语言等所有方面发现远超人类所及之物的东西 —— 需要三年，比他原先的三到五年缩短了，理由是编码 agent 能做的事。关于监控，他说所有人都依赖的那种基于语言的方法正在触到边界：监控者面对的是上百万字的推理，或者是系统想一套做一套的主动欺瞒，因此他提出 shadow actions（影子动作）—— 让 agent 在你能检查的地方把动作做出来，但不让这个动作真正落地。他们的承诺是：真实世界里的任何动作，都要以这门超级智能科学给出的证据为闸门。这些全都不是测量，全是预测或设计原则。

The premise is that coding is no longer the bottleneck at Anthropic; the developer infrastructure built for human scale is. The series is the core of it: in May 2026 they averaged around 2,500 code changes merged per day, and as of September they merged over 30,000 per day. They built Scion, an in-house version control system, and migrated the monorepo off git in five months, shipping a code review surface called Antique, code search, and a source control IAM system inside the same period.

The git numbers explain why. Main-branch history alone is almost 100 GB and a compressed working copy is over 25 GB; they perform on the order of 25 million code checkouts per day for CI, before counting developer environments, agent sandboxes and research jobs, which fetched naively would be over 600 PB of git traffic per day. At peak they ran several hundred internal git mirrors. Scion inverts two of git's assumptions: clients are always online, because nearly all clients are agents and jobs, and the server rather than the client answers history and blame, so the client keeps only a bounded local cache. Google's Piper and Meta's Sapling are cited as prior art.

The result is given as two ratios: Scion's infrastructure footprint is now 200x smaller than the previous git mirroring setup in CPU and memory, achieved while the volume of code merged per day grew 10x. Both are first-party, both are internal, and neither has an external reproduction — which is also why this post belongs in the same issue as Box's eval deltas and Sottiaux's disk reduction.
